0:004 min

Hook: the search box that lies to you

Goal  They feel the problem in their gut before they hear a single definition.

Do not open with “today we’ll learn about embeddings.” Open with a broken product. Share your screen on a plain text file or slide showing a help-centre FAQ list, and a search box you type into.

“Quick show of hands in the chat — who has ever searched a company’s help page, known for a fact the answer was in there, and still got nothing? Type 1 in the chat.”

Let the 1s roll in for ten seconds. Then show it happening.

“Here’s a support page with eight articles. I’m a user. I type what’s actually in my head: I can’t sign in to my account. And the search gives me… ‘How do I delete my account permanently?’ I asked for help getting in. It offered to destroy my account.”

That laugh is your buy-in. Now name the villain.

“The search wasn’t broken. It did exactly what it was built to do — match the letters I typed. The only word my sentence shares with any article is ‘account’. It has no idea that sign in and password live in the same universe. It matches spelling. I wanted it to match meaning.”

Ask the chat

“So how would you fix this? Drop one idea in the chat.” You will get “synonyms” and “a dictionary” — use them in the next block.

Why the obvious fix doesn’t scale

Goal  Close the escape hatches so the real solution feels necessary, not arbitrary.

Take the chat’s answers seriously and kill them one at a time. This is the move that separates a teacher from a slide-reader.

  • “Add a synonym list.” Fine — you write it. login, log in, log-in, sign in, signin, sign-in, can’t get in, locked out, credentials not working. Now do that for every concept in your product, in every language, forever. And it still fails on a phrasing nobody predicted.
  • “Use a thesaurus / WordNet.” Better, but it’s word-by-word. It has no view on whether a whole sentence means the same thing as another whole sentence.
  • “Just use ChatGPT.” Someone will say this. Good — ask them how the model knows which eight articles to look at. Park it: “hold that thought, it’s where we finish.”

Then state the session’s question and leave it on screen.

“So the real question for the next forty minutes is this: how do you get a computer — which only does arithmetic — to decide that two sentences with no words in common mean the same thing?”

0:078 min

Meaning as coordinates

Goal  “Embedding” stops being jargon and becomes an obvious idea they could have had themselves.

Start somewhere they already know cold. Colour.

“Forget AI for a second. How does a computer store the colour red? Three numbers — 255, 0, 0. And here’s the part that matters: 254, 2, 1 is also red. Nobody told the computer those are both red. It falls out of the fact that the two sets of numbers are close together. We turned a human concept into coordinates, and closeness in those coordinates became similarity. That is the entire trick we’re about to play on language.”

Now build it up on words, one dimension at a time. Draw this live if you can — a whiteboard, or just two axes on a slide.

  • Take one made-up dimension: “how food-ish is this word?” 0 to 1. Place pizza at 0.95, biryani at 0.95, laptop at 0.05.
  • Now coffee and Java both score high on food-ish. Problem. So add a second dimension: “how programming-ish?” Now Java separates from coffee, because it sits high on both.
  • Land the generalisation: one number can’t hold a meaning. Two is better. The model we’ll use has 384.
Ask the chat

“If I plot coffee, tea, and laptop on these two axes — which two end up as neighbours?” Everyone gets it right. That’s the point: they just did an embedding lookup in their head.

Now correct the one thing they’re about to get wrong, because it’s the question that always comes.

“Obvious next question: who sat down and defined those 384 dimensions? Nobody. That’s the whole point. The model was trained on an enormous amount of text with a stupidly simple job — guess the missing word. To get good at that, it had no choice but to invent its own internal dimensions of meaning. And here’s the honest part: we mostly can’t read them. Dimension 137 doesn’t say ‘food-ish’. It says 0.0413, and nobody knows exactly why. Those learned coordinates are what we call an embedding.”

Be honest

The king/queen analogy is a real property but a heavily cherry-picked one; most word-arithmetic you try yourself will disappoint. Say that out loud. Nothing buys credibility with a sharp fresher faster than volunteering the limits of your own demo.

0:156 min

Cosine similarity, worked by hand

Goal  They can compute a similarity score on paper. Then util.cos_sim is a convenience, not a black box.

They have coordinates. Now they need a measuring tape. Start with the wrong tool, because the comparison is what teaches.

“Obvious idea: measure the straight-line distance between two points, like school geometry. It works… until it doesn’t. Imagine a two-line tweet about cricket and a four-thousand-word article about cricket. Same topic, wildly different length — so their vectors point the same way, but one arrow is much longer than the other. Straight-line distance says they’re far apart. They’re not. They’re just different sizes.”

“So we throw length away and keep only direction. Measure the angle between the two arrows. That’s cosine similarity. Zero degrees — same direction — scores 1.0. Ninety degrees, unrelated, scores 0. Opposite, −1.”

Now do the arithmetic in front of them with tiny fake vectors. Two dimensions: “access-ish” and “food-ish”. Nobody gets bored during four multiplications, and it permanently kills the idea that this is magic.

"log in" = [0.9, 0.1] "sign in" = [0.8, 0.2] "pizza" = [0.1, 0.9] cos(A,B) = (A . B) / (|A| * |B|) log in vs sign in A . B = 0.9*0.8 + 0.1*0.2 = 0.74 |A| = sqrt(0.81 + 0.01) = 0.906 |B| = sqrt(0.64 + 0.04) = 0.825 cos = 0.74 / 0.747 = 0.99 log in vs pizza A . B = 0.9*0.1 + 0.1*0.9 = 0.18 |A| = 0.906 |B| = 0.906 cos = 0.18 / 0.82 = 0.22
0.99 vs 0.22 — and not one shared letter did any work.

“That’s it. That’s the algorithm. Multiply, add, divide. Everything else today is the same four lines of arithmetic, just with 384 numbers instead of 2 — and with the numbers learned by a model instead of made up by me.”

Expect this

Someone will ask what score counts as “similar”. Answer: there is no universal threshold — it depends on the model and your data, so you calibrate it on your own examples. Practitioners rank by score and take the top-k far more often than they threshold. Saying “you tune it, here’s how” is a better answer than inventing 0.7.

0:213 min

From words to whole sentences

Goal  Justify sentence-transformers before they see the import line.

One gap left to close, and it sets up the library choice.

“So far I’ve talked about single words. But my user typed a sentence. Easy fix — average the word vectors? Try it: dog bites man and man bites dog. Same three words, so the average is identical. Two very different days, one vector. Averaging throws away word order, and word order is where a lot of meaning lives.”

“So instead we use a model that reads the sentence as a sentence — every word in the context of the others — and emits one vector for the whole thing. That’s a sentence embedding model. The one we’re using is all-MiniLM-L6-v2: about 80 MB, runs on this laptop with no GPU, and gives us 384 numbers per sentence.”

Then set the stage for the demo so they know what they’re watching for.

“Four things now, live. First I’ll show you the actual numbers. Then that similar things score high. Then I’ll fix the broken search from the start of the session. And then — because you should never trust a demo where everything works — I’ll show you how to break it.”

Look at that second score: 0.459 against a winner of 0.502. Uncomfortably close. Change the phrasing slightly and the wrong answer wins outright — measured, not hypothetical.

“I changed ‘can’t log in’ to ‘locked out’ — same meaning to you and me — and the wrong article jumped to first place. So retrieval is not a solved problem. This is exactly why production systems don’t stop at cosine similarity: they take the top twenty and hand them to a slower, smarter reranker that actually reads each candidate against the question. Cosine similarity is the cheap first pass, not the final answer.”

Worth the minute

Run 4 — now break it on purpose  3 min

Most demo sessions stop at the win. Ending on a limitation is what makes you read as a practitioner rather than someone who followed a tutorial last night.

demo 4 · the failure mode
pairs = [
    ("The flight was on time",  "The flight was delayed"),     # opposites
    ("I love this product",     "I hate this product"),        # opposites
    ("I love this product",     "This product is wonderful"),  # genuinely similar
]

for a, b in pairs:
    s = util.cos_sim(model.encode(a), model.encode(b)).item()
    print(f"{s:.3f}   {a!r}  vs  {b!r}")
verified output
0.800   'The flight was on time'  vs  'The flight was delayed'
0.698   'I love this product'     vs  'I hate this product'
0.829   'I love this product'     vs  'This product is wonderful'

Read the first and last lines together and let the room sit with it: a pair of opposites at 0.800, a hair under a genuine match at 0.829.

“‘On time’ and ‘delayed’ mean opposite things, and they’re scoring 0.800 — essentially tied with a pair down there that genuinely does match, at 0.829. If you built a flight-status checker on this, it could not tell you whether your flight was fine or ruined. Why? Because these vectors capture what a sentence is about far better than which side of it you’re on. ‘On time’ and ‘delayed’ appear in nearly identical contexts across the training text, so they land next to each other. Never build a sentiment classifier out of raw cosine similarity — and if your search has to respect a ‘not’, embeddings alone will not save you.”

“Every technique you learn has an edge like this. The engineers who get hired are the ones who know where the edge is.”

0:383 min

You just built the engine inside RAG

Goal  Convert a neat trick into something they recognise from job posts. This is the block that makes them stay for your next session.

Now cash in the “just use ChatGPT” comment you parked at minute five.

“Someone asked earlier why we don’t just hand this to an LLM. Here’s the answer. An LLM has a limited context window and it has never read your company’s documents. You cannot paste ten thousand pages into it. So how does a ‘chat with your PDF’ product actually work? It does exactly what we just did.”

Walk five steps. Keep it as five words on screen, not a diagram you have to explain.

  1. Chunk — split the documents into paragraph-sized pieces.
  2. Embed — run every chunk through the model we just used. Same line of code.
  3. Store — keep the vectors in FAISS, Chroma, pgvector, Pinecone.
  4. Retrieve — embed the user’s question, cosine-similarity it against everything, take the top 5. This is literally Run 3.
  5. Generate — paste those 5 chunks into the prompt: “answer using only this context.”

“That’s Retrieval-Augmented Generation. The R and the A — the retrieval, the part that decides what the model is even allowed to look at — is the thing we spent the last forty minutes on. The LLM is the last step, and honestly the easy one. When a RAG chatbot gives a stupid answer, it is usually not the LLM’s fault. It’s that retrieval handed it the wrong five chunks.”

Close with a twenty-second name-drop, so it stops feeling academic: Spotify recommendations, YouTube’s next video, Google understanding a badly-typed query, duplicate-ticket detection, resume–JD matching, and every “chat with your docs” startup of the last two years. Fast list, no elaboration — the five steps above are what carries this block.

“And remember what we saw in Run 3b — when retrieval picks wrong, the LLM confidently answers the wrong question. That’s most ‘the AI hallucinated’ complaints in production. It’s a retrieval bug wearing a costume.”

Questions you will get

Six of these are near-certain with this audience. Have the answers loaded — especially the honest “we don’t fully know” ones.

Do I need a GPU?

No. Everything in this session runs on CPU in a couple of seconds. MiniLM is ~80 MB. You need a GPU for training large models, not for embedding a few thousand sentences with a small pretrained one.

What do the 384 numbers actually mean?

Individually, mostly nothing we can name. They’re learned, not designed. Interpretability research does find readable directions inside these spaces, but you should not expect dimension 137 to have a clean English label. What’s reliable is relative position: which vectors sit near which.

Say “we don’t fully know” where it’s true. It teaches them that honesty is part of the job.

How is this different from Word2Vec / GloVe?

Those give one fixed vector per word, so bank gets the same vector in “river bank” and “bank loan”. Transformer-based models are contextual — the vector changes with the surrounding words — and sentence-transformers are additionally fine-tuned so that whole-sentence vectors are directly comparable with cosine similarity.

Does this work for Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Bengali?

Not with the model in this demo — MiniLM is English-focused. You want a multilingual model such as paraphrase-multilingual-MiniLM-L12-v2 or a LaBSE-family model. Worth knowing that with a good multilingual model, the same sentence in two languages lands in nearly the same place — so you can search Hindi documents with an English query. That fact reliably gets an audible reaction.

What if I have a million documents? Isn’t comparing against all of them slow?

Yes, and that is exactly what vector databases exist for. Brute-force cosine over everything is fine up to roughly tens of thousands of items. Past that you use approximate nearest-neighbour indexes (FAISS, HNSW) which trade a sliver of accuracy for enormous speed. Good moment to name-drop FAISS, Chroma, pgvector, Pinecone, Qdrant — these appear in job descriptions.

Which model should I pick?

Don’t guess from a leaderboard alone — but do point them at the MTEB leaderboard on Hugging Face as the standard starting reference. Practical advice: start with the smallest model that clears your bar, measure on your data with your queries, and only then pay for something bigger. Bigger embeddings cost storage, latency, and money on every single query.

If cosine similarity can rank the wrong thing first, how do real systems cope?

Run 3b earns you this question — take it, it’s a good one. Three answers, in the order teams actually reach for them:

Rerank. Retrieve the top 20 by cosine, then pass each candidate through a cross-encoder that reads query and document together. Far slower per pair, so you only run it on 20, not on a million. Big accuracy win.

Hybrid search. Combine semantic scores with old-fashioned keyword scoring (BM25). Each catches what the other misses — exact product codes and names are where keyword still wins outright.

Better chunking. Unhelpfully boring and usually the biggest single lever in a real RAG system. If your chunks straddle two topics, no amount of model quality rescues the retrieval.

Is this what ChatGPT does?

Related but not the same. Embeddings are one vector per piece of text, used for comparison. A generative LLM uses similar internal representations but is trained to produce the next token. The connection to draw is the RAG one: embeddings choose what the LLM gets to see.
